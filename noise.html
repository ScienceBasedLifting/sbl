Editorial

Not every debate matters for your gains.

The evidence-based lifting space is full of arguments. Some are genuinely contested science. Some are content theater. Here is how to tell the difference so you can stop doom-scrolling and start training.

The 90/10 rule

  • 90% of your results come from basics: train hard, eat enough protein, sleep well, progress over time.
  • The remaining 10% is where real debates live — and where noise pretends to be signal.
  • If you have been training less than 2 years, almost nothing on this page affects your gains.
  • Learn the fundamentals first, then come back for nuance.

Category 1

Theater — content designed to generate engagement, not learning

Exercise tier lists and "best exercise" rankings

Nearly every "best chest exercise" video is engagement bait. Exercise selection matters, but the ranking is highly individual — it depends on your anatomy, injury history, and available equipment. A creator who says "incline dumbbell press is the best chest exercise, period" is making content, not coaching you.

  • The real question is not "what is the best exercise" but "what is the best exercise for your structure."
  • Creators who teach you how to evaluate exercises (Joe Bennett, Kassem Hanson) are more useful than those who hand you a list.

"Science says" one-study takes

A single study does not settle anything. When a creator cites one paper and declares a technique proven or debunked, they are either oversimplifying for content or do not understand how evidence accumulates. The real signal comes from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, not individual RCTs.

  • Watch for: "A new study just proved..." — this is almost always premature.
  • Better framing: "This study adds to a body of evidence suggesting..." — this is how researchers talk.

Physique-based authority

Looking great does not mean someone understands the science of why they look great. Genetics, drug use, years of training, and good photography explain most elite physiques. When an enhanced bodybuilder tells you to do 30 sets per muscle per week, that is their drug-supported recovery capacity talking, not evidence-based programming.

Category 2

Marketing — real information wrapped in a sales funnel

"The one thing you are doing wrong" hooks

Fear-based content creation. The format is: identify an anxiety, amplify it, present a solution (usually a program or coaching service). The information inside might be fine, but the framing is designed to make you feel broken so you buy something.

  • If a creator makes you feel worse about your training before offering a paid fix, that is a funnel.
  • Good educators make you more capable of training yourself, not more dependent on them.

Supplement-driven content

When a creator's content consistently leads to supplement recommendations, check who sponsors them. The supplements with strong evidence for lifters are short: creatine monohydrate, caffeine, and arguably protein powder for convenience. Everything else is marginal at best.

  • If someone tells you to take 10 supplements, they are selling supplements.
  • If someone builds an entire philosophy around supplementation and "optimization," be skeptical of the evidence base.

Category 3

Genuinely contested — real debates worth following

These are not noise

Some debates in the evidence-based lifting community are legitimate. The research is incomplete, smart people disagree, and the answer genuinely matters for how you train. These are worth your time if you are past the beginner stage.

  • Lengthened partials vs full ROM — active research area with real implications for exercise selection
  • High-volume vs intensity-first programming — legitimate disagreement about optimal set counts
  • Frequency ceilings — does training a muscle 3-4x/week beat 2x/week?
  • Mind-muscle connection — does it actually drive hypertrophy or just feel better?
  • Motor-unit recruitment as a programming variable — emerging concept with limited evidence

We cover all of these with both sides represented on the Debates page.

The bottom line

If you have been training for less than two years, the noise-to-signal ratio in fitness content is brutal. You do not need to have an opinion on lengthened partials. You need to train consistently, eat enough protein, sleep well, and progressively overload. The creators in our directory are selected because they teach useful things — but even the best creators produce some content that is more entertainment than education.

Your filter: does this content change what I do in the gym tomorrow? If not, it is interesting but not urgent. Save it for later.

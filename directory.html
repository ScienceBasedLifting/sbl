Why start here

These creators solve the most common problem new lifters face: information overload. JPG Coaching's rapid-fire "Do This, Not That" corrections give you instant improvements between sets. Keenan Malloy answers the first programming question every beginner asks ("what split should I run?") with a defensible full-body approach. Scientifically Jacked bridges the gap between the science-heavy content below and everyday gym life.

The community-tier creators — rafmurdock, doom_mxn, trainedby_kashi, trainerqa, cooperlogann, and Jacob Oestreicher — represent the next wave of evidence-based voices. They're translating established science into formats that meet lifters where they already are: short-form reels, meme-literate commentary, and coaching-style Q&A.

Once the basics feel automatic, move into the Exercise Science section to understand the "why" behind the cues — that's where King Deltoids, Davis Diley, YoTalks, and the deeper biomechanics creators live.

What this group teaches you

This is where the "why" lives. Jewers, TNF, and Paul Carter argue that only the final reps near muscular failure drive growth — everything before is junk volume. They favor machines over barbells because stability lets the target muscle reach failure without stabilizer exhaustion. Kassem Hanson and Joe Bennett add individualization: your anatomy determines which exercises are optimal for you.

Jeff Nippard and Milo Wolf provide the academic counterweight. Nippard blends literature reviews with practical programming. Wolf's ROM research clarifies that mechanical tension at long muscle lengths drives growth — not "stretch" itself, a distinction the influencer ecosystem frequently oversimplifies. Ben Yanes bridges both camps, warning against the jargon overload that leads to paralysis by analysis.

King Deltoids narrows the lens to shoulder hypertrophy with biomechanically-justified exercise picks. Davis Diley adds practical exercise cueing and a "less is more" recovery-first philosophy. YoTalks provides accessible exercise-selection debate commentary — though his motor-unit framing should be cross-referenced with MuscleMechanicsLab. Trell Bankston represents the intensity-first camp alongside Paul Carter — the TBJP-style counterpoint to high-volume orthodoxy.

The addition of Mike Israetel, Greg Nuckols, Eric Helms, Menno Henselmans, and MuscleMechanicsLab broadens this section into the most comprehensive exercise science roster available. Israetel's volume-landmark system is the most widely adopted hypertrophy framework in evidence-based lifting. Nuckols and Helms provide the deepest programming and research foundations. Henselmans challenges conventional hypertrophy prescriptions with meta-analytical rigor. MuscleMechanicsLab pushes into advanced biomechanics territory for the most committed learners.

Why nutrition gets its own section

Most fitness content treats nutrition as an afterthought — "just eat in a surplus." The creators in this section push back on that. Ella Parungao (Scientific Snitch) shows that lipogenesis and muscular hypertrophy operate on distinct energetic pathways, arguing for conservative surpluses over aggressive bulking. John Jewett brings the rare combination of IFBB Pro competition and a registered dietitian credential, bridging competitive practice with clinical nutrition science. Eric Trexler provides the deepest academic grounding through published research on energy availability and metabolic adaptation.

Layne Norton's PhD work on protein metabolism and leucine thresholds has genuinely shaped evidence-based nutrition practices, even if his combative delivery style divides opinion. Together, these creators cover nutrition from beginner-accessible explainers through to primary-research-level depth — the full pipeline a natural lifter needs to make informed dietary decisions rather than following generic "eat big" advice designed for enhanced athletes.
